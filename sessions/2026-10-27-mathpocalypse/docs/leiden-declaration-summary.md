# Research note: the Leiden Declaration on AI and Mathematics

**Source:** [Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics](https://leidendeclaration.ai/)
— dated 2 June 2026; [arXiv:2609.35334](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35334);
DOI [10.5281/zenodo.20302944](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.20302944)

**Added:** 18 October 2026

> Summary of the declaration's website as of 18 October 2026. The signatory count changes over time.

## What it is

A community call to action on the challenges AI poses to mathematical research, endorsed by the
International Mathematical Union (IMU). It reflects AI tools and mathematical practice as of May 2026.
It does **not** oppose AI: "Mathematicians have a choice about whether and how to adopt artificial
intelligence." Its focus is on disclosure, human responsibility and attribution.

**Origin.** It grew out of the Lorentz Center (Leiden) workshop *Mechanization and Mathematical
Research* in September 2025, which had ~60 participants from 10 countries. A 16-member working group,
convened by Jim Portegies (TU Eindhoven), then drafted it over eight months with community feedback.

## Values to preserve

1. **Proof:** proofs confer "the highest degree of certainty" and explain *why* results hold.
2. **Attribution:** results belong to named authors who take credit and responsibility for them.
3. **Transparency:** arguments must be verifiable without "proprietary knowledge or equipment".
4. **Evaluation:** work is judged by "shared standards of depth, difficulty, and significance".
5. **Autonomy:** researchers shape the direction of research and the methods used to pursue it.

## Threats identified

1. **Unreliable arguments:** AI can produce plausible but flawed proofs, which strains peer review.
2. **Attribution erosion:** model outputs often fail to cite the human work they synthesise.
3. **Distorted incentives:** AI use may be rewarded for its own sake, which disadvantages people who lack access.
4. **Informal announcements:** results announced by press or blog before community evaluation can
   overstate what the AI did.
5. **Loss of autonomy:** research questions may be chosen because they suit automation, and
   partnerships with industry may be unequal.

## Recommendations (abridged)

- **Individual mathematicians:** disclose tool use; keep responsibility for correctness; affirm the
  human nature of authorship; attribute properly; follow open-science principles; support reviewers; stay
  informed; choose tools carefully; weigh the ethics of your work.
- **Organisations and not-for-profit funders:** lead on publishing and reviewing policy; keep standards
  of rigour; protect authors' rights; support public research labs; make funding match these values.
- **Policymakers:** protect authors' rights; "Don't believe the hype"; regulate the AI industry;
  invest in public computational infrastructure.
- **Commercial AI:** collaborations with mathematicians should meet the declaration's standards, and
  companies should respect employees' freedom to speak openly about corporate policies.

## Endorsements

- **Institutions:** IMU; ICARM (Carnegie Mellon); the Mathlib Initiative.
- **Featured individuals:** Peter Scholze, Terence Tao, Robbert Dijkgraaf, Ilka Agricola, Jeremy Avigad,
  Kevin Buzzard, Leslie Ann Goldberg, Steven Strogatz.
- **Signatories:** 4,355 as of 17 October 2026 ([list](https://leidendeclaration.ai/signatories)).

## Relevance to the session

- Threats 1 and 4 apply directly to the OpenAI results: announced through informal channels, with
  proofs that humans have largely not read. Is a Lean certificate enough to satisfy the "Proof" and
  "Transparency" values?
- "Disclose tool use" and "affirm the humanity of authorship" raise a question: who is the author of
  ~400 AI-generated solutions?
