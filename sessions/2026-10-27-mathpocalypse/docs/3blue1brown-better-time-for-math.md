# Research note: 3Blue1Brown, "There's never been a better time to go into math"

**Source:** [There's never been a better time to go into math](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc)
on the 3Blue1Brown YouTube channel (Grant Sanderson). The video had about 1.8M views when this note
was added.

**Added:** 18 October 2026

> These notes cover only the section from
> [20:46](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1246s) onwards. That section asks how
> mathematical research, and the job of a human mathematician, changes once AI models can produce
> complex proofs ([21:28](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1288s)). They are summary
> notes with timestamps, not a transcript.

## 1. Proof generation vs theory building

[21:43](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1303s)–[23:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1421s)

- **Proofs used to be the currency of research.** Proving theorems has long been the main goal of
  mathematical research and the way it measures success. Once AI can produce formal proofs quickly,
  the speaker argues, a proof no longer works well as a stand-in for human understanding.
- **Treat proofs as "raw data".** Proofs become the input to further work rather than the end
  product ([23:20](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1400s)).
- **The creative work moves to theory building.** The valuable part becomes finding the simplicity
  underneath a set of proofs and fitting them into a coherent framework. The examples given are
  Évariste Galois creating group theory and Alexander Grothendieck transforming algebraic geometry
  ([21:10](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1270s)).

## 2. Communication and oral presentation

[24:32](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1472s)–[25:11](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1511s)

- **A machine-made proof still has to be explained.** Making a proof understandable, motivated and
  meaningful to other people takes human communication.
- **Presenting in person matters more.** Oral presentation and sharing ideas face to face become
  more important skills. Maths rests on shared human intuition and culture, and people come to care
  about a concept when they see someone who genuinely cares about it
  ([25:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1506s)).

## 3. Asking the right questions and making conjectures

[25:16](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1516s)–[26:06](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1566s)

- **Taste in questions.** A core research skill is deciding which questions are worth asking: the
  taste to state powerful conjectures. The video introduces this idea much earlier
  ([03:34](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=214s)).
- **Humans still do the interpreting.** AI may start to help with, or suggest, fruitful conjectures.
  Even so, humans are still needed to interpret where those conjectures lead, connect them to wider
  science, and build the theory that eventually produces real-world applications
  ([25:55](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1555s)).

## 4. The changing culture and experience of research

[26:26](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1586s)–[31:09](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1869s)

- **Researchers react in opposite ways.** Some feel disheartened because being the first to crack a
  particular proof is less of a thrill
  ([31:14](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1874s)). Others are exhilarated to get an
  early look at answers to hard questions they have always wanted resolved
  ([30:08](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1808s)).
- **New directions open up.** These include theory driven by humans, more weight on the aesthetic
  pleasure of learning and exploring ideas, and academic rewards that favour deep conceptual
  understanding over raw proof output
  ([30:58](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKrwhjOI8Nc&t=1858s)).

## Relevance to the session

- **An optimistic counterweight to the dread.** It answers the "Mathpocalypse" anxiety directly:
  proofs getting cheap shifts what mathematicians do rather than ending the job. Set it beside the
  Fields Medallists' [statement](mathandai-statement-summary.md) and the
  [Solve Everything](solve-everything-summary.md) blueprint.
- **Proofs as data, understanding as the product.** This fits the
  [Aaronson interview](aaronson-modern-cto-interview.md), where Lean confirms that proofs are
  correct but nobody has necessarily understood them. Generating results is cheap; understanding
  and explaining them is not.
- **A ready-made discussion prompt.** "If proofs are raw data, what is the research output?" works
  for an RSE audience, because code produced by agents raises the same question for software.
- **The "disheartened vs exhilarated" split** is a low-stakes way to bring out how the group feels.
